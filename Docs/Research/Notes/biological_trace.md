# Detection, collection and packaging of biological and trace evidence at a burglary scene (CSI practice, for a VR jewellery-store simulation)

Research method note for the report writer: from this environment only web-search summaries were reachable; every attempt to open a full document (FBI Handbook of Forensic Services, FSR-GUI-0016, DC DFS KM SOP, NIJ course pages, NIST IR 7928, BlueStar user guide, San Diego PD biology manual) failed at the network layer (DNS/proxy 403). All findings below are therefore drawn from search-engine excerpts of those documents, which carried quoted text and URLs. Where an excerpt was clearly from the primary document it is cited as such; where only a secondary or low-authority source (vendor page, flashcard site, encyclopedia) was available this is stated. Figures that could not be confirmed are listed under Gaps rather than asserted. Jurisdictional variation is flagged "(US)" or "(UK)" where the source is clearly one or the other.

## 1. Blood: visual search and presumptive tests (Kastle-Meyer, LMG, Hemastix, TMB)

### Takeaway
At scene level the CSI first searches visually (white light, oblique light, then 415 nm violet to darken stains), then runs a presumptive catalytic test on a moistened swab or a Hemastix strip. Kastle-Meyer (phenolphthalin) and LMG are the more specific reagents, TMB/Hemastix the more sensitive; all are presumptive only, read within ~10 s, require positive and negative controls, and give false positives with plant peroxidases and chemical oxidants.

### Cited Findings
- Kastle-Meyer chemistry: "relies on the peroxidase-like activity of hemoglobin in blood to catalyze the oxidation of phenolphthalin (the colorless reduced form of phenolphthalein) into phenolphthalein, which is visible as a bright pink color." — [Wikipedia, Kastle-Meyer test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kastle%E2%80%93Meyer_test)
- Basic KM procedure as reported: wipe the suspected stain with a cotton swab, add a drop of phenolphthalin reagent, then after a few seconds a drop of hydrogen peroxide; some labs first add a drop of ethanol "in order to break open the cells." — [Science Notes, Kastle Meyer Test for Blood](https://sciencenotes.org/kastle-meyer-test-for-blood/); [Science Buddies project page](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/science-fair-projects/project-ideas/BioChem_p037/medical-biotechnology/crime-scene-chemistry-kastle-meyer-test-for-blood)
- Two-stage reading: if colour appears after the reagent step but before peroxide, the test is read as negative/invalid "due to the presence of chemical oxidants contaminating the sample." Reagent order also varies between labs (peroxide before or after phenolphthalein). — [AAFS 2011 abstract A139](https://www.aafs.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/AAFS-2011-A139.pdf); [Rutgers Camden lab handout](https://graduateschool.camden.rutgers.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2024/04/27.pdf)
- Timing: "A positive result appears as a pink color change within 10 seconds of adding hydrogen peroxide"; "waiting for a period of time over 30 seconds will result in most swabs turning pink naturally as they oxidize on their own in the air." — [Science Notes](https://sciencenotes.org/kastle-meyer-test-for-blood/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kastle%E2%80%93Meyer_test)
- False positives: plant peroxidases (horseradish given as an example), other haemoglobin-containing material, and reagent degradation (phenolphthalin re-oxidises in light/air, so store in opaque sealed containers). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kastle%E2%80%93Meyer_test); [LegalClarity summary](https://legalclarity.org/kastle-meyer-test-for-blood-how-it-works-in-forensics/)
- Controls: a known blood sample (positive) and a clean unstained sample (negative) run alongside every test to verify the reagents. — [Science Notes](https://sciencenotes.org/kastle-meyer-test-for-blood/)
- A DC Department of Forensic Sciences SOP "FBS02 KM Testing" exists (not opened). — [DC DFS SOP](https://dfs.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dfs/page_content/attachments/FBS02%20KM%20Testing.pdf)
- Comparative sensitivity/specificity (Cox 1991, J Forensic Sci): "The phenolphthalein and the leucomalachite green tests are the most specific, and the tetramethylbenzidine and orthotolidine tests are the most sensitive of the group"; LMG "the least sensitive of the four". — [NCJRS abstract 132525](https://www.ncjrs.gov/App/Publications/abstract.aspx?ID=132525)
- Tobe et al. 2007 (J Forensic Sci), six presumptive tests incl. Hemastix: LMG detected blood to 1:10,000; the remaining tests (including Hemastix) to 1:100,000 dilution; also examined effect on high-molecular-weight DNA. — [Strathprints record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/10091/); [USM record of the 4-test study](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/7082)
- A Forensic Science International study (Peru) comparing TMB, o-toluidine, LMG and BlueStar against blood and blood-like products found LMG "the reagent with less false positives". — [UPCH repository](https://repositorio.upch.edu.pe/handle/20.500.12866/4809)
- Hemastix field method (vendor description): a drop of distilled water on the strip, rub the strip on the sample, compare colour to the chart on the bottle; "commonly used at crime scenes to determine whether the evidence seen is actually blood spatter, before any lab analysis". Detects "the peroxide-like activity of hemoglobin". — [Ward's Science Hemastix](https://www.wardsci.com/us/en/product/8876724/hemastix)
- Hemastix limitations (low-authority flashcard source): cannot distinguish human from animal blood; approx. one-year shelf life; a control test needed first use or if >24 h since last control on that container. — [Brainscape crime scene manual cards](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/crime-scene-manual-11650034/packs/20511722)
- Blood and 415 nm: blood "does not show significant fluorescence but does have a strong absorption band around 395–435 nm, with a maximum at 415 nm", so 415 nm gives bloodstains the most contrast; blood "is the only body fluid that does not fluoresce". — [Horiba application note, detection of body fluids with an ALS](https://www.horiba.com/int/scientific/applications/others/pages/detection-of-body-fluids-with-an-alternate-light-source/); [Labino forensic filters guide](https://www.bergeng.com/mm5/downloads/labino/Labino-Forensic-Filters.pdf)
- Phoenix Crime Lab ALS chart lists a 415 nm violet band "for blood prints and gunshot residue" and a 350 nm UV band for stains and fingerprints. — [Phoenix PD crime lab document 1700](https://crimelab.phoenix.gov/QTDocuments/1700.PDF)

### Inferences
- For the VR task flow: white-light/oblique search -> 415 nm violet with yellow/orange goggles to make stains look black -> photograph with scale -> presumptive test on a swab taken from the stain edge (so the main stain is preserved for collection) -> collect. The "test the edge, not the centre" step is standard practice but was not confirmed in an opened primary source (see Gaps).
- A simulation should model the two-stage KM read (colour before peroxide = invalid), the ~10 s positive window, and a positive/negative control step, since all three are documented and are the typical trainee errors.

### Gaps
- Could not open an SOP (DC DFS FBS02, FBI, UK) to quote exact drop counts, swab-moistening fluid, or the rule to sample the stain periphery. Jurisdiction-specific choice of reagent (UK forces commonly KM; US agencies vary KM/LMG/Hemastix) could not be sourced.
- Tobe 2007 effect on DNA (which reagents degrade high-molecular-weight DNA) is in the paper but not in the excerpt.

## 2. Latent blood: luminol and BLUESTAR (application, darkness, photography, what they destroy or preserve)

### Takeaway
Chemiluminescent reagents are sprayed as a fine mist from about 50 cm in darkness, with a known-blood positive control and an adjacent negative control; the glow lasts about a minute and can be refreshed by re-spraying; it is photographed on a tripod with long exposures. Multiple studies find luminol/BlueStar do not degrade STR DNA typing, but they are presumptive only and give false positives with some food residues, metals and oxidisers.

### Cited Findings
- BLUESTAR user guide: the reagent glows without total darkness, "but total darkness makes faint, high-dilution traces much easier to find"; wait about five minutes for the eyes to adjust; "spraying lightly from at least 50 cm, sweeping side to side"; "designed to be vaporized from waist height in a fine mist". — [BLUESTAR Medium Kit User Guide (2023)](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/USER_GUIDE_medium_kit_EN_20231511.pdf)
- Controls: test the solution on a known blood sample as a positive control and spray a nearby unstained area as a negative control. — [BLUESTAR, Use at a suspected crime scene](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/use-of-bluestar-at-a-suspected-crime-scene/)
- Photography: vendor DSLR settings ISO 800, f/8, 8 s on a tripod in a darkened room with diffused light; natural light preferred over artificial, which "can give images an unnatural green or yellow tint". An older guide gives ISO 400, ~f/2.8, bulb exposure, no flash, "focus the lens manually over a spot of light provided by a flashlight over the blood area"; another suggests bracketing shutter speeds, typically 30 s. — [BLUESTAR, Photographing bloodstains](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/2016/07/); [BLUESTAR tablet user leaflet](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/user-bl-4-tablet.pdf)
- Duration/refresh: "The glow fades after about one minute but can be refreshed by spraying again." — [BLUESTAR Medium Kit User Guide](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/USER_GUIDE_medium_kit_EN_20231511.pdf)
- Limitations and confirmation: food residues and some chemicals produce false positives; positives should be confirmed with an immunochromatographic test (OBTI or Identi-HEM named) and finally by a laboratory. — [BLUESTAR, Use at a suspected crime scene](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/use-of-bluestar-at-a-suspected-crime-scene/)
- Effect on DNA, Gross, Harris & Kaldun 1999 (J Forensic Sci): luminol "did not adversely effect the PCR testing and did not interfere with the PT and TMB presumptive tests"; "the substrate and the method of cleaning were the major factors affecting DNA yield". — [NCJRS abstract](https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/effects-luminol-subsequent-analysis-bloodstains)
- Laux 1991: no effect on presumptive test, Takayama confirmatory test or ABO typing, but both luminol preparations affected certain (enzyme) genetic markers. — [FIU GFJC literature copy](https://archive.gfjc.fiu.edu/workshops/resources/literature/The%20Effect%20of%20Luminol%20on%20Presumptive%20Tests%20and.pdf)
- Almeida, Glesse & Bonorino 2010 (Forensic Sci Int): "48h after benzidine treatment, but not luminol or Bluestar solution application, sample DNA was degraded". — [PubMed 20643520](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20643520)
- Blum et al. 2006 (Can Soc Forensic Sci J 39(3):81-100) introduced BLUESTAR and reported no harmful effect on DNA profile determination (manufacturer-hosted copy). — [BLUESTAR-hosted paper](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/csfs_vol39_bluestar_blum.pdf); [STR validation study, manufacturer-hosted](https://www.bluestar-forensic.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/str_validation_study.pdf)
- A 2004 ISFG congress paper also covers luminol formulations and DNA profiling. — [ISFG 2004 paper](https://www.isfg.org/files/ea84de9e210d90fc6b60188b862dcc26772e235c.03015395_285078074257.pdf)

### Inferences
- In a jewellery-store burglary the main latent-blood use case is a cut offender who wiped or cleaned a surface near broken glass; the simulation should require blackout (shop lights off, shutters down or night scene), tripod, control spray, and re-spray for photography, and should score a false-positive trap (bleach cleaning residue or copper fittings) as a known limitation.
- Because chemiluminescent reagents dilute and spread a stain, the documented best practice is to photograph/record visible staining and sample it before spraying; this ordering is implied by the vendor guidance but was not quoted verbatim (Gap).

### Gaps
- Exact luminol mixing (sodium perborate/sodium carbonate concentrations) and the common false-positive list (bleach/hypochlorite, copper, horseradish) were not returned in quotable form by the searches run.
- Whether UK forces routinely use BlueStar vs luminol at volume-crime scenes was not sourced.

## 3. Collecting wet and dry blood; bloodstain-pattern basics at scene-officer level

### Takeaway
Dried stains are collected on a sterile swab moistened with distilled water (not saline), concentrating the stain on the tip, optionally followed by a dry swab, with a control swab from an adjacent unstained area; swabs are air-dried before going into paper (never plastic), one stain per swab, one swab set per envelope. Scene officers record pattern type (drip/passive, transfer, cast-off) and measure width and length of elliptical stains for angle of impact (sin θ = w/l), excluding the tail.

### Cited Findings
- Moistening: "If the stain is dry, moisten a sterile cotton swab with distilled water from an ampoule; avoid using saline. Do not touch the ampoule to the swab, or the stain. Instead drop the water onto the swab or stain from a distance of about ½ of an inch." — [NIJ course, dry stains on non-absorbent surfaces](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/collecting-dna-evidence-at-property-crime-scenes/evidence-collection/dry-stains-non-absorbent-surfaces)
- Concentrating the sample: "An effort should be made to concentrate the stain in a localized portion of the swab or pad. For example, when using a swab, the stain should be concentrated on the tip." — [SJSU DNA Sample Handling notes](https://gcp-web.sjsu.edu/people/steven.lee/courses/c2/s1/DNA_Sample_Handling%201.pdf)
- Second swab: "Some laboratories recommend following the first moistened swabbing with a second dry swabbing to ensure thorough sample collection. Both swabs are retained and submitted for analysis." — [FIU/NIJ property crimes module](https://archive.gfjc.fiu.edu/property_crimes/module03/pro_m03_t13.htm)
- Control swab: "Collect the control sample by slightly moistening the swab with distilled water and rubbing an area of the surface in an unstained region near the stain", then air-dry, label and package in paper. — [NIJ course](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/collecting-dna-evidence-at-property-crime-scenes/evidence-collection/dry-stains-non-absorbent-surfaces)
- Wet stains on non-absorbent surfaces have a separate NIJ/FIU module. — [FIU module, wet stains](https://archive.gfjc.fiu.edu/property_crimes/module03/pro_m03_t11.htm)
- Drying: "samples should be left open to the atmosphere and allowed to air dry before they are packaged in paper envelopes"; "Packaging a wet swab into a plastic tube with holes or a pipet tip will considerably slow the drying process." — [SJSU DNA Sample Handling](https://gcp-web.sjsu.edu/people/steven.lee/courses/c2/s1/DNA_Sample_Handling%201.pdf)
- Paper not plastic: "Never store blood, whether wet or dry, in a plastic evidence container. Always use a paper bag or paper evidence envelope. Each item should be labeled and packaged individually. It is important not to place more than one sample into an evidence envelope and never collect multiple stains on the same swab." — [LibreTexts, Bloodstain collection (Hartnell College course)](https://workforce.libretexts.org/Courses/Hartnell_College/The_Introduction_to_the_Forensic_Sciences/14%3A_Evidence_Collection_and_Preservation/14.03%3A_Bloodstain_collection)
- Virginia DFS has a procedure "FB PM Screening and Collection for DNA Analysis" (210-D2003), not opened. — [Virginia DFS procedure](https://dfs.virginia.gov/wp-content/uploads/210-D2003%20FB%20PM%20Screening%20and%20Collection%20for%20DNA%20Analysis-4043-661fda1256e06.pdf)
- Packaging bloodstained items: "Wet and dried items should not be folded over on themselves, and paper wrapping can protect bloodstain patterns"; items should be inventoried and packaged before leaving the scene. — [NIJ course, biological evidence packaging](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/collecting-dna-evidence-at-property-crime-scenes/evidence-collection/biological-evidence-packaging); [NIJ demonstration video](https://nij.ojp.gov/media/video/31361)
- Angle of impact: "sin θ = width / length, where θ represents the angle of impact"; the tail is excluded from the length; worked example 3.0 cm long x 1.5 cm wide gives sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30°; porous surfaces (carpet) distort stains. — [Alabama ACCESS forensic science lesson 06-04](https://accessdl.state.al.us/AventaCourses/access_courses/forensic_sci_v11/forensic_ua_v11/6_unit/06-04_lesson.htm); [calculate.co.nz angle of impact calculator (educational only)](https://www.calculate.co.nz/angle-of-impact-calculator.php)
- Terminology (ASB Technical Report 033, 2017): "cast-off pattern: A bloodstain pattern resulting from blood drops released from an object due to its motion"; "drip pattern: A bloodstain pattern resulting from a liquid that dripped into another liquid, at least one of which was blood"; "drip stain: a bloodstain resulting from a falling drop that formed due to gravity"; SWGSTAIN adds "Cessation Cast-off Pattern ... due to its rapid deceleration". — [ASB TR 033 e1 2017](https://www.aafs.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/033_TR_e1_2017.pdf); [NIST-hosted SWGSTAIN BPA terms (draft)](https://nist.gov/system/files/documents/2017/11/15/bpa_terms_and_definitions_in_bloodstain_pattern_analysis.pdf)
- Passive vs transfer are textbook force-based categories: passive stains "formed by gravity as the only external force", transfer stains where "the principle acting force to cause the stain is adhesion only". — [eCampusOntario, Language of Forensics: Bloodstain Pattern Evidence](https://openlibrary-repo.ecampusontario.ca/jspui/bitstream/123456789/1052/2/Language-of-Forensics-Bloodstain-Pattern-Evidence-1646922834.odt)
- Hemospat maintains a BPA terminology list. — [HemoSpat terminology](https://hemospat.com/bloodstain-pattern-analysis-terminology/?term=all)

### Inferences
- Scene-level recording for a burglary is limited to: photograph each stain group with scale and a north/orientation marker, note substrate, classify as drip/transfer/cast-off/wipe, measure the long and short axes of a few elliptical drops; angle calculation and reconstruction are analyst work (the sources describe the formula as taught content, and one calculator explicitly disclaims forensic use).
- A control swab from the adjacent clean surface is a consistent, scorable step across US training material and should be mandatory in the simulation.

### Gaps
- UK scene practice (e.g. whether the control/background swab is routinely taken at volume crime) was not sourced; the material above is US (NIJ/FIU) and college courses.
- "Swab box" terminology: no source described a swab box; US sources use paper envelopes with swab dryers/holders. UK forces commonly use cardboard swab boxes with a tamper label, but no citable source was returned.
- Bevel & Gardner and James & Nordby textbooks were not accessible.

## 4. Touch DNA: where it is left, swabbing technique, contamination avoidance, quantity needed, persistence, secondary transfer

### Takeaway
In burglaries touch DNA is sought on door/window handles and frames, tools, broken glass edges, display-case surfaces and abandoned items. The double-swab (wet then dry) method is the standard many forces use; 30 s of swabbing improves recovery on fabric. Results depend heavily on substrate, contact type and the individual ("shedder status"), and 37% of glove-handled tools still gave the earlier bare-hand user's profile. Secondary transfer is real and must be considered when interpreting a profile from a glove or handle.

### Cited Findings
- Double-swab origin (Sweet et al. 1997, J Forensic Sci 42(2):320-322): a wet cotton swab followed by a dry one; of three methods compared it "recovered the highest percentage of saliva from skin". — [NCJRS abstract](https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/improved-method-recover-saliva-human-skin-double-swab-technique)
- Mechanism (secondary explanation): moisture from the first swab rehydrates and loosens dried epithelial cells; the dry second swab picks them up. — [ForensicField, saliva examination](https://forensicfield.blog/saliva-examination-in-forensic-science/)
- Swabbing technique vs substrate (UTS): a wet-moist double swab for 30 seconds maximised DNA recovery from cotton; on tile a single wet or moist-dry swab maximised recovery. — [UTS, Effect of swabbing technique and duration on forensic DNA recovery](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/170325/3/Effect%20of%20swabbing%20technique%20and%20duration%20on%20forensic%20DNA%20recovery.pdf)
- Alketbi (UCLan), validating touch-DNA collection with cotton swabs: lists the wet-and-dry double swab as a way to improve trace DNA recovery; "letting a swab dry before extraction usually leads to some loss of DNA" (lab-side point). — [UCLan CLoK](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/id/eprint/37761/1/Validating%20Touch%20DNA%20Collection%20Techniques%20Using%20Cotton%20Swabs.pdf)
- A J Forensic Sci 2021 touch-DNA study: ~13.7% more offender DNA with the double swab but no higher STR recovery; several methods "perform equally well on human skin". The double swab nonetheless "became the standard in DNA sampling used by many police forces and labs worldwide". — [JFS 10.1111/1556-4029.14733 (mirror)](https://onlinelibrary-wiley-com-443.webvpn.zafu.edu.cn/doi/10.1111/1556-4029.14733)
- Burglary tools (Pfeifer & Wiegand 2017, Int J Legal Med): 234 STR analyses; when a second user handled the tool bare-handed the first user was attributed in 1 of 40 cases; when the second user wore gloves, "the first user matched the DNA handle profile in 37% of the cases"; results depend "mainly on the type of contact, the handle material, and the individual user". — [Springer Medizin record](https://www.springermedizin.de/persistence-of-touch-dna-on-burglary-related-tools/12075566)
- Residential burglary door handles (Swiss, Forensic Sci Int: Genetics Suppl. 2022): at LR threshold 1000, "43 of the 70 residents could be detected on the door handles"; about 10% of major components came from unknown individuals (visitors, perpetrator or secondary transfer). — [BORIS Bern open copy](https://boris.unibe.ch/176278/1/1-s2.0-S1875176822000178-main.pdf)
- Vehicle sites (Aidarous et al. 2025, 1,769 samples, 359 vehicles): steering wheel highest success, then gear shift; exterior and interior door handles "substantially lower recovery". — [UCLan CLoK 56484](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/id/eprint/56484)
- Persistence factors: NIJ-funded 2023 article on how surface type, environmental condition and exposure time affect touch DNA stability; a review notes recovery from "nonporous substrates, particularly metals commonly encountered in forensic settings (e.g., weapons, door handles, tools), is especially problematic". — [NIJ, Persistence of Touch DNA for Forensic Analysis](https://nij.ojp.gov/index%2Ephp/library/publications/persistence-touch-dna-forensic-analysis-article); [Atlantis Press review](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126023894.pdf)
- Gloves and transfer: Carrara et al. 2023 (Forensic Sci Int: Genetics), "DNA transfer when using gloves in burglary simulations"; Otten et al. and Tanzhaus et al. "observed indirect DNA transfer on items of interest through different types of gloves", with the glove-wearer's shedder status affecting results; "surgical gloves can transfer DNA of innocent persons to a crime scene". — [FSI:G 2023 paper (mirror)](https://roebuckforensics.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/1-s2.0-S1872497322001648-main.pdf); [AAFS 2010 abstract A62](https://www.aafs.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/AAFS-2010-A62.pdf)
- Glove interiors as DNA source: a 2006 AAFS abstract describes gloves from abandoned stolen vehicles "sampled by cutting or swabbing the interior surfaces". — [AAFS 2006 B31](https://www.aafs.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/AAFS-2006-B31.pdf)
- Legal caution on a glove profile: Indiana Court of Appeals reversed a burglary conviction where a glove stain matched the defendant because "there was no evidence that would support an inference that Meehan's DNA was found on the glove because he handled it during the burglary, as opposed to some other time" (later vacated on transfer to the Supreme Court; final outcome not in results). — [FindLaw, Meehan v. State](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/in-supreme-court/1665077.html)
- Shedder status: literature has moved from good/poor to high/intermediate/low shedders; Goray et al. 2016 examined 240 handprints from 10 people over times and days; secondary transfer "skin-to-object-to-skin or skin-to-skin-to-object" is described as still an open question. — [Univ. Catania literature review 2023](https://www.iris.unict.it/retrieve/aabe4d42-7813-4fa5-83af-add9d990005d/2023_Indirect%20DNA%20Transfer%20and%20Forensic%20implications%20a%20literature%20review.pdf); [AAFS 2020 B115](https://www.aafs.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/AAFS-2020-B115.pdf)
- Low-template limits: with 34 PCR cycles "it is possible routinely to analyse <100 pg DNA", with increased heterozygote imbalance and stutter (Gill et al. 2000, Forensic Sci Int). — [PubMed 10882828](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10882828/)
- General kit template guidance 500-1000 pg; a flashcard source states DNA-17 "requires about 80 cells (<500 picograms) for a standard result" (unverified, low authority). — [Brainscape, Generating a DNA profile](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/generating-a-dna-profile-8829458/packs/15141316)
- Glove changing (US lab policy as analogue to scene practice): FBI Laboratory EVDC-320-06: staff "will change gloves between each proximal container when directly handling individual items of evidence if transfer of trace, biological, or other materials is a concern"; DC DFS: "gloves shall be changed between cases, and items when DNA collection is a consideration". — [FBI EVDC-320-06 Minimizing Contamination](https://fbilabqsd.fbi.gov/file-repository/evidence-management/evdc-320-06-minimizing-contamination.pdf); [DC DFS LFU02 SOP manual](https://dfs.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dfs/publication/attachments/LFU02-Laboratory_Standard_Operating_Procedure_Manual.pdf)
- Collection priority at property crimes (NIJ): biological fluids, trace materials and fragile evidence first, then "swabs of handled or moved items", then lower-quality biological evidence; local lab/agency procedures may change this. — [NIJ course, collection priority](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/collecting-dna-evidence-at-property-crime-scenes/collection-priority); [NIJ, location and collection of evidence](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/crime-scene-and-dna-basics-forensic-analysts/evidence-crime-scene/location-collection-evidence)
- Dusting for prints before trace collection can move trace evidence or contaminate it with powder; process for prints after trace. One agency general order (Paradise Valley PD) instead sequences "marked, photographed, fingerprinted and DNA-swabbed" before collection, so order varies by agency. — [guide excerpt](https://web4.paprikaapp.com/activa/18500503); [Paradise Valley PD GO 83.3](https://public.powerdms.com/ParadiseValley/documents/454725/83.3%20Collection%20Of%20Evidence%20At%20The%20Scene)

### Inferences
- For a jewellery-store scene, likely touch-DNA targets are: the inside/outside of the entry point (rear door handle, shutter, forced window frame), the display-case glass edges and sliding-door runners, any tool (pry bar, hammer) left behind, and abandoned gloves (swab inside fingertips/cuff). The data suggest tools and glove interiors outperform exterior metal handles.
- The simulation should enforce a new pair of gloves per item/area, a mask (see section 9), swab moistened with sterile water, firm rotating pressure for ~30 s, dry swab follow-up, air-dry, paper/swab packaging, and labelling before moving on.
- A scorable "interpretation" point: a profile from a handle or glove shows presence, not timing or activity (Pfeifer & Wiegand; Meehan).

### Gaps
- Exact pressure/rotation wording from a primary SOP and the "no talking/coughing over items" rule were not returned in quotable form (FSR-GUI-0016 sections 4.1-4.2 reportedly contain the scene rules, but the PDF could not be opened).
- No validated minimum template figure for DNA-17 (or NGM/Fusion/ESX) from a regulator or vendor validation document; only general 500-1000 pg kit guidance and an unverified "80 cells" claim.
- Persistence in days/weeks for indoor handles was not quantified in the excerpts returned.

## 5. Hairs and fibres: finding, lifting, packaging (druggist/bindle fold); fibre transfer at glass entry points

### Takeaway
Hairs and fibres are located with oblique white light and violet/blue ALS (some hairs fluoresce), lifted singly with clean forceps onto trace paper or recovered en masse with low-tack tape lifts; loose traces go into a druggist (bindle) fold and then an envelope, never loose into an envelope and never tape-sealed inside the fold. Fibres are commonly left on woodwork or glass at a burglary entry point, but no experimental study of fibre-to-glass-edge transfer was found.

### Cited Findings
- Forceps and paper: "Through use of clean forceps and paper (i.e., trace paper), the sample can be removed from the surface and placed into a clean piece of paper that can be folded and packaged in a paper envelope." — [FIU/NIJ property crimes module T08](https://archive.gfjc.fiu.edu/property_crimes/module03/pro_m03_t08.htm)
- Disposable tweezers work well; Post-it notes can hold hairs lightly by their adhesive (fire-investigator guide). — [IAAI evidence guide, hair](https://www.iaaievidenceguide.com/hair)
- Tape lifting: trace tape is used for hairs and fibres not visible to the eye; "use only low tack tapes and keep the lifts from sticking to anything else"; vacuuming is least preferred because of cross-contamination risk; adequate reference samples must also be submitted. — [California DOJ Physical Evidence Bulletin 6 (trace)](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/media/peb-06.pdf)
- Bindle: "carefully fold the paper, together with the comb/brush, into a paper bindle to prevent loss of any trace evidence and place the bindle in an envelope". — [California DOJ PEB 6](https://www.oag.ca.gov/sites/all/files/agweb/pdfs/cci/reference/peb_6.pdf)
- Druggist fold rule (Maine State Police, updated Feb 2025): "do NOT place hairs or fibers directly into an envelope. Always use a druggist fold"; and do not tape-seal the druggist fold itself. — [Maine State Police Druggist Fold Guide](https://www.maine.gov/dps/msp/sites/maine.gov.dps.msp/files/inline-files/Druggist%20Fold%20Guide.pdf)
- Tape limitations: tapes "cannot be used on surfaces where they cannot be easily removed, such as on paper and other easily damaged or delicate surfaces". — [US patent 8234940 background](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8234940B2/en)
- UK-style kit guidance: tape lifting "used to collect traces of physical evidence from exhibits, particularly fibres"; items involved in transfer "should be considered for fibre evidence irrespective of whether they are visible to the naked eye or not". — [NSW Special Commission exhibit (NSW Police kit document)](https://lgbtiq.specialcommission.nsw.gov.au/assets/lgbtiq/hearings/20230704/exhibits/Exhibit-51/Exhibit-51-Tab-002P-NPL.9000.0003.1395.pdf)
- ALS for hairs: "some human and animal hair will fluoresce brightly under Violet and/or Blue illumination". — [Foster + Freeman Crime-lite application notes](https://fosterfreeman.com/?p=18173)
- Fibres at entry points (practitioner statement): fibres "may be left at a crime scene, for example being found on woodwork or glass at the point of entry in a burglary"; evidential strength "depends upon the number and type of fibres recovered". — [Key Forensic Services, Fibres](https://keyforensic.co.uk/defence/our-services/trace-evidence/fibres)
- Reverse transfer (glass onto clothing), Luce et al. 1991: 86% of backward-fragmentation fragments landed directly below the frame, letting the CSI place a suspect on one side of the glass; clothing type and wetness affect fragment retention; most fragments are lost "within a matter of a few hours". — [NCJRS abstract, backward fragmentation](https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/study-backward-fragmentation-window-glass-and-transfer-glass); [British Glass digital library](https://www.britglass.org.uk/knowledge-base/digital-library-and-information-services/transfer-glass-part-2-study-trasnfer-glass); [Cellmark glass casework](https://www.cellmarkforensics.co.uk/services/forensic-casework/glass/)
- A 2023 Forensic Sci Int paper examined glass transfer to dampened recipient garments. — [UTS record](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/175124)

### Inferences
- For the VR scene: the broken display-case/window edge should carry visible snagged fibres (and possibly blood) found with oblique torchlight; the correct action is photograph with scale, lift with clean forceps into a druggist fold, then envelope, label; or submit the whole glass fragment in a rigid box (section 8). Tape-lift the floor/frame area for invisible fibres; keep lifts on their acetate/backing, not folded on themselves.
- Reference ("control") fibres from the store's own carpets/uniforms are needed to exclude innocent sources.

### Gaps
- Step-by-step druggist-fold diagram text and the "tape onto clear acetate sheet" wording could not be quoted (Maine PDF not opened).
- No experimental study on fibre transfer from clothing to fractured glass edges was found.

## 6. Saliva and abandoned items (cigarette ends, drink cans, masks/balaclavas, gloves, caps) as DNA sources

### Takeaway
Cigarette ends, drink containers, masks and headgear are high-value saliva/wearer-DNA sources; they are picked up with clean gloves or sterile forceps, each item packaged separately in a paper envelope/bag, and exposed items are at risk of environmental DNA degradation. Research-grade handling detail exists; a quotable field SOP was not reachable.

### Cited Findings
- Cigarette butts "may be used to identify persons and link them to a crime through DNA profiling of epithelial cells from saliva stains"; butts are "often exposed to chemical contaminants and environmental conditions which lead to DNA degradation". — [Science Diliman (UP) study](https://journals.upd.edu.ph/index.php/sciencediliman/article/download/6743/5842)
- Lab handling: researchers "used sterile forceps to handle each butt before cutting the filter paper for extraction". — [Univ. Bologna 2025 paper](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/b58144b5-0107-43bb-a9eb-1c983977a8e4/s12024-025-01151-z.pdf)
- Historical packaging example: butts "each contained in a separate sealed white envelope" (NSW case file). — [NSW Special Commission exhibit](https://lgbtiq.specialcommission.nsw.gov.au/assets/lgbtiq/hearings/20231010/exhibits/Exhibit-64/Exhibit-64-Tab-039-NPL.0131.0001.1909.pdf)
- Case use: police matched DNA from a discarded cigarette butt to crime-scene DNA; courts have scrutinised covert collection (Iowa water-bottle swap rejected; North Carolina patio butt ruled off-limits). — [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/cigarette_butts_and_used_kleenex_become_a_crime-solving_dna_tool)
- Common biological sources listed by a lab best-practice document include clothing, bedding, ligatures, drinking containers. — [Best practices for collection, packaging, storage... (LAB-BP-01)](https://crime-scene-investigator.net/PDF/best-practices-for-collection-packaging-storage-preservation-and-retrieval-of-biological-evidence.pdf)
- Glove interiors sampled by cutting or swabbing (see section 4). — [AAFS 2006 B31](https://www.aafs.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/AAFS-2006-B31.pdf)
- A latex glove on a bedroom floor yielded a CODIS-matched profile in a Pennsylvania burglary arrest. — [CrimeWatch PA report](https://delaware.crimewatchpa.com/netherprovidencepd/16127/arrests/carrozza-carmen-n-1-count-18-3502-a2-f1-burglary-overnight-accommodation-and-3)
- AAFS feature on a rubber glove leaving "genetic fingerprints" at a scene. — [AAFS research feature](https://aafs.org/research/we-dont-catch-smart-ones-how-rubber-glove-left-genetic-fingerprints-crime-scene)
- NIJ-funded evaluation of transfer of saliva-derived DNA (relevant to masks/balaclavas and secondary transfer). — [NIJ record](https://nij.ojp.gov/index%2ephp/library/publications/evaluation-transfer-saliva-derived-dna-0)

### Inferences
- In the simulation: a cigarette end outside the rear door, a drink can on the counter, and a balaclava/cap dropped in flight are each a separate exhibit: photograph in situ, pick up with forceps or fresh gloves touching the minimum area (not the filter tip/rim/mouth area), paper envelope or bag, seal, label, biohazard marking. Wet items (can with liquid) are drained or dried per section 8.
- Saliva/mask items should be marked for ALS screening (saliva fluoresces under UV/violet/blue; section 7) only if the stain location is uncertain; otherwise submit whole.

### Gaps
- No reachable SOP quoting the "handle by the unburnt end / do not touch the rim" rule or the preferred packaging (paper envelope vs small box) for cans.

## 7. Alternate light sources: wavelengths, goggles, what fluoresces, operation

### Takeaway
Scene ALS work uses UV ~350-380 nm, violet ~415 nm, blue 450 nm and blue-green ~485-505 nm bands with yellow or orange barrier goggles (orange/OG550 for 450 nm blue). Semen, saliva, urine and sweat fluoresce under UV/violet/blue; blood does not fluoresce but absorbs strongly at 415 nm and appears black; photographs use the same colour filter as the goggles. The lights are eye/skin hazards and exposure should be limited.

### Cited Findings
- 450 nm with orange: San Diego PD forensic biology manual lists "Crime-lite 80S Blue (450 nm)" and "Blue/Green (485nm)" with "Orange goggles or filters"; Crime-lite ML Blue (450-510 nm) uses an OG550 orange filter, ML Blue/Green (430-470 nm) a yellow GG595 filter; "these high-intensity sources can harm eyes and skin, and that even with goggles, exposure time should be limited". — [San Diego PD Forensic Biology Unit Technical Manual (2019)](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/fbunittechnicalmanualv08-12-2019.pdf); [2011 edition](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2011fbunittechnicalmanual.pdf)
- Horiba: stains on cloth "Visualized in Blue light (450nm) with an Orange camera filter"; longer-wavelength illumination with its filter "starts to reduce (reject) the background and increase the fluorescence of the stain"; blood has "a unique wavelength under which the blood stain will darken to enhance its contrast"; detergent brighteners fluoresce near 415 nm so use a filter or a source above 440 nm to suppress background. — [Horiba application note](https://www.horiba.com/int/scientific/applications/others/pages/detection-of-body-fluids-with-an-alternate-light-source/)
- Commercial kit: 450 nm LED torch plus orange goggles "for detecting body fluids, fluorescent powders, and trace evidence". — [Arrowhead Forensics 450 nm kit](https://arrowheadforensics.com/450nm-led-forensic-light-source-kit/)
- Crime-lite 42S band pairs: UV 350-380 nm with violet; blue 420-470 nm with blue/green 445-510 nm; green 480-560 nm with orange 570-610 nm; supplied with twin-filter viewing goggles. — [Foster + Freeman Crime-lite 42S](https://fosterfreeman.com/?p=4735); [range page](https://fosterfreeman.com/?p=27233)
- Targets: "body fluids including semen, sweat, saliva and urine can all be seen to fluoresce brightly under UV, Violet and Blue illumination"; some hair fluoresces under violet/blue; bone and tooth under blue, blue/green or green. — [Foster + Freeman application notes](https://fosterfreeman.com/?p=18173)
- Phoenix Crime Lab guideline: photograph using "the same color of lens as the goggles you were wearing when the evidence was best viewed"; a filtration band "should not be eliminated without first attempting to use it"; 350 nm UV (80 nm width) for stains and fingerprints; 415 nm violet for blood prints and GSR. — [Phoenix PD crime lab doc 1700](https://crimelab.phoenix.gov/QTDocuments/1700.PDF)
- Blood absorbs UV and appears black rather than glowing. — [Labino forensic filters](https://www.bergeng.com/mm5/downloads/labino/Labino-Forensic-Filters.pdf)
- Polilight use for semen detection (older validation literature). — [FIU GFJC literature, Polilights](https://archive.gfjc.fiu.edu/workshops/resources/literature/Screening/42_The%20Use%20of%20Polilights%20in%20the%20Detection%20of%20Seminal.pdf)

### Inferences
- A VR ALS mechanic: darken room; select band; goggle colour is forced to match (yellow for UV/violet, orange for blue, red for green); body fluids and some fibres glow, blood turns dark at 415 nm; camera filter equals goggle colour; a timer or warning for prolonged exposure. Fibres and fingerprint powders fluoresce under blue/blue-green; brightener-treated fabrics glow and are a false-positive trap.

### Gaps
- Precise fluorescence emission wavelengths for semen/saliva and the specific red-goggle pairing for 505-530 nm were not returned; the 365 nm figure for UV torches appeared only as the general 350-380 nm band.

## 8. Packaging, labelling, drying, chain of custody, exhibit referencing

### Takeaway
Biological items go in paper (bags, envelopes, boxes), never sealed plastic; wet items are air-dried (hung or flat, indoors, away from the scene, or in a filtered drying cabinet) and if not fully dry are put in paper so drying continues; sharps go in boxes or breathable tubes marked "sharp"; each item is packaged separately and marked biohazard where applicable. Labels carry case number, item number, date, collector's initials (US) or a unique exhibit reference built from the officer's initials and a sequential number (UK), and tamper-evident bags/labels carry unique barcodes recorded for chain of custody.

### Cited Findings
- Paper vs plastic: crime-lab guidance favours "paper bags, envelopes, or boxes" and advises against plastic because "sealed plastic lets moisture build up and encourages bacteria and mold". — [Iowa DPS crime lab evidence guide](https://dps.iowa.gov/media/111/download); [LAB-BP-01 best practices](https://crime-scene-investigator.net/PDF/best-practices-for-collection-packaging-storage-preservation-and-retrieval-of-biological-evidence.pdf)
- Drying: wet items "can be air-dried by hanging them or laying them flat on a clean surface indoors, away from the scene"; where full drying is not possible "a paper bag lets the item keep drying in packaging"; biological material degrades so wet items must dry or reach the lab quickly. — [LAB-BP-01](https://crime-scene-investigator.net/PDF/best-practices-for-collection-packaging-storage-preservation-and-retrieval-of-biological-evidence.pdf); [Lake County (OH) Crime Lab submission procedures](https://www.lakecountyohio.gov/lakecountyprosecutor/wp-content/uploads/sites/56/2022/03/LCCL_Evidence_Submission_Procedures-1.pdf)
- Sharps: "Sharp items go in boxes or breathable tubes, and the packaging should be marked to say a sharp object is inside"; package each item separately; "mark the packaging and tell the lab if a biohazard is present". — [LAB-BP-01](https://crime-scene-investigator.net/PDF/best-practices-for-collection-packaging-storage-preservation-and-retrieval-of-biological-evidence.pdf); [Iowa DPS](https://dps.iowa.gov/media/111/download); [California DOJ evidence collection reference](https://www.oag.ca.gov/sites/all/files/agweb/pdfs/cci/reference/evidence_collection.pdf)
- Drying cabinets: enclosed, HEPA-plus-carbon filtered cabinets for drying wet/bloody items, to protect staff from pathogens and odours and reduce cross-contamination (vendor literature). — [AirClean DrySafe](https://www.officer.com/investigations/forensics/forensic-lab-equipment/product/10709522/airclean-systems-drysafe-drying-cabinet); [Mystaire SecureDry](https://www.officer.com/investigations/forensics/product/10775841/mystaire-securedry-forensic-evidence-drying-cabinet)
- US label fields (NIJ): direct marking can interfere with analysis, so soil, hair and stains go into a container that carries "the agency case number, item number, date recovered or received, and the investigator's initials"; package before leaving the scene. — [NIJ course, biological evidence packaging](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/collecting-dna-evidence-at-property-crime-scenes/evidence-collection/biological-evidence-packaging)
- NIST IR 7928 Biological Evidence Preservation Handbook (2013, 20-member working group): five sections (retention, safe handling, packaging and storage, chain of custody and tracking, disposition); Appendix C is a sample chain-of-custody report; Figure II-1 is a biohazard label; handlers should assume material is hazardous (bloodborne pathogens). — [NIST IR 7928 PDF](https://nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/NIST-IR-7928.pdf); [Colorado CBI copy](https://cbi.colorado.gov/sites/cbi/files/NIST%20-%20The%20Biological%20Evidence%20Preservation%20Handbook.pdf); [NIJ library entry](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/biological-evidence-preservation-handbook-best-practices-evidence-handlers)
- UK (Home Office CFI) exhibit reference: three initials of the seizing officer (plus a surname letter if only two names), an 8-digit date and a sequential number from 1, e.g. "ABC25062023-1"; split items "ABC25062023-1-A, -1-B, -1-C". — [Home Office, Tamper evident bags (accessible)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/tamper-evident-bags/tamper-evident-bags-accessible-version)
- UK tamper-evident bag: "a transparent, leak-proof plastic bag with a strong adhesive seal"; bag, seal and label combined; the seal shows the word "stop" if tampered; bags are barcoded and the unique number must be recorded "to keep the chain of evidence intact". — [Home Office TEB guidance v6/2023 PDFs](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68d292c0275fc9339a248b9b/Tamper+evident+bags.pdf); [accessible version](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/tamper-evident-bags/tamper-evident-bags-accessible-version)
- UK commercial supply: SceneSafe sells uniquely numbered, barcoded tamper-evident bags, a tamper-evident CJA exhibit label, and "KeepSafe Breathable" bags; older card tags on string ties "can be lost or altered". — [SceneSafe CJA label](https://scenesafe.co.uk/pages/new-tamper-evident-cja-exhibit-label-from-scenesafe); [KeepSafe Breathable Bag](https://scenesafe.co.uk/products/keepsafe-breathable-bag-20-5-x-29); [Perforation TEB](https://scenesafe.co.uk/products/perforation-tamper-evident-bag)
- UNODC "Crime scene and physical evidence awareness for non-forensic personnel" (ST/NAR/39, 2009) includes a section on transportation, storage and submission of evidence. — [UN Digital Library PDF](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/659283/files/Crime_scene.pdf)
- Massachusetts State Police evidence submission handbook exists (not opened). — [Mass.gov Evidence Submission Handbook](https://www.mass.gov/doc/evidence-submission-handbook/download)

### Inferences
- UK vs US difference to model: UK practice is a plastic TEB (leak-proof, barcoded) for most exhibits but breathable/paper for wet biological items; US practice is paper for all biological evidence with a tamper-evident tape seal and the collector's initials across the seal. Both: one item per package, label before sealing, record the unique seal number in the scene log.
- The UK police "CJA label" (Criminal Justice Act exhibit label) format is the realistic UK scene artefact; the Home Office initials-date-number format above is specific to Home Office CFI teams, and police forces use their own schemes (typically officer initials/sequence such as "ABC/1").

- Exact fields on a UK police CJA exhibit label and a force-level numbering convention were not returned by search (only the Home Office CFI scheme).
- The FBI Handbook of Forensic Services packaging tables (e.g., "air dry; package in paper; do not use plastic"; sharps in boxes) could not be opened to quote.
- NIST IR 7928 recommended storage temperatures (e.g., frozen/refrigerated/room-temperature by evidence type) are in the handbook but not in the excerpts.

## 9. Common contamination errors and how laboratories detect them (UK regulatory frame)

### Takeaway
The main contamination routes are personnel-to-exhibit, consumable-to-exhibit and exhibit-to-exhibit; controls are PPE (suit, mask, double gloves changed per item), ISO 18385 DNA-grade consumables, separate packaging, and staff/elimination DNA databases. Laboratories detect contamination through negative controls, elimination-database matches and batch-tray audits, as the Adam Scott case (re-used plastic tray, DNA in the negative control ignored) showed.

### Cited Findings
- FSR-GUI-0016 "Contamination controls - Scene of crime" (Issue 1, October 2023, under the Forensic Science Regulator Act 2021) covers scene contamination controls and scene activities/procedures. — [FSR-GUI-0016 PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/65362ef6e839fd000d867325/FSR-GUI-0016_DNA_contamination_controls_-_incident_scenes.pdf)
- Predecessor FSR-G-206 "The Control and Avoidance of Contamination In Crime Scene Examination involving DNA Evidence Recovery" (2015 consultation; Issue 2 later cited); section 11.1.5a concerns inclusion on the Contamination Elimination Database (CED) from October 2018. — [FSR-G-206 consultation draft](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/393866/206_FSR_SOC_contamination_consultation.pdf); [FSR Newsletter 34 (April 2020)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5eaade2ae90e0723bad309d6/FSR_NL_No34_April2020_v1.pdf)
- UK DNA contamination detection protocol lists the governing standards: FSR-G-206, PAS 377, BS ISO 18385:2016 and FSR-G-208 (laboratory), and identifies routes "from personnel to exhibit/DNA sample; from contaminated consumables (for example, swabs, tubes) to exhibit/DNA sample". — [Gov.uk, DNA contamination detection (accessible)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dna-contamination-detection/dna-contamination-detection-accessible)
- ISO 18385:2016 covers manufacture of products used to collect, store and analyse forensic DNA material, including "evidence-collection sampling kits such as swabs, containers and packaging", lab plasticware, disposable lab coats and gloves; it obliges manufacturers to reduce detectable human nuclear DNA in products; a committee draft (ISO/CD 18385.2) will replace it. — [SCC record ISO 18385:2016](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/8158171); [ISO/CD 18385.2](https://www.iso.org/standard/90626.html)
- FSR 2026 notification: a risk-based, milestone pathway to Code v2 compliance for incident scene examination, suspending the ISO/IEC 17020 accreditation deadline of 6 April 2027. — [FSR-NOT-01-2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a3e6bb2d52550a19950f60e/FSR-NOT-01-2026_-_INC_100_Milestones_1_and_2.pdf)
- Adam Scott case (FSR report FSR-R-618, 17 Sept 2012): contamination "through the incorrect re-use of disposable plastic trays between 8 and 10 October 2011 during the automated DNA extraction process at LGC"; Scott's saliva from an unrelated spitting-incident sample "had been present in one cell of a tray that was re-used"; the batch "showed DNA present in the negative control, but this was not sufficiently dealt with"; "human error by a technician who failed to follow basic procedures for the disposal of plastic trays"; charged 23 Oct 2011, held ~5 months, charges dropped March 2012; ~26,000 samples processed by that lab since March 2011. — [FSR DNA contamination report (gov.uk)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7b470f40f0b66eab9a02b7/dna-contam-report.pdf); [Chemistry World](https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/forensic-lab-error-led-to-miscarriage-of-justice-/5503.article); [Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/?p=106738)
- Glove-change rules in US lab policy (FBI EVDC-320-06; DC DFS LFU02) as cited in section 4. — [FBI EVDC-320-06](https://fbilabqsd.fbi.gov/file-repository/evidence-management/evdc-320-06-minimizing-contamination.pdf); [DC DFS LFU02](https://dfs.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dfs/publication/attachments/LFU02-Laboratory_Standard_Operating_Procedure_Manual.pdf)
- UK FFLM "Recommended equipment for obtaining forensic samples" (Jan 2024) lists kit for medical forensic sampling (relevant to DNA-grade swab specifications). — [FFLM Jan 2024](https://fflm.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Recommended-equipment-for-obtaining-forensic-samples-Prof-M-Stark-Jan-2024.pdf)
- A Forensic Capability Network SARC newsletter reports a PPE study (not opened). — [FCN SARC news issue 4](https://fcn.police.uk/docs/issue-04-sarc-news-ppe-study-v20-2pdf/download)
- Secondary summary of contamination reduction measures. — [AZoLifeSciences, Reducing Contamination in Forensic Investigations](https://azolifesciences.com/article/Reducing-Contamination-in-Forensic-Investigations.aspx)

### Inferences
- Trainee errors worth scoring in the VR scene: not wearing a mask while swabbing; reusing gloves across exhibits; placing two items in one bag; touching the swab tip or the ampoule to the surface; packaging a wet swab/garment in sealed plastic; talking/coughing over an open exhibit; leaning across the entry point without a suit; not taking a control swab; not sealing/labelling before moving to the next item; folding a bloodstained garment on itself. All but the talking/coughing rule are directly supported by the cited material; the latter is standard but was not returned verbatim.
- Laboratory detection of scene contamination relies on the staff/CSI elimination database (UK CED) and negative controls; a VR debrief can show a "CED hit" to the trainee's own profile as the consequence of a mask/glove failure.

- No quantitative figure for how often scene-officer contamination is detected via elimination databases.
