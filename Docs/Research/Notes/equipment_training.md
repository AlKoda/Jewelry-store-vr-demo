## Key question 1 — Field-kit inventory: what is in a CSI's kit, what each item looks like (sizes, colours, brands) and how it is used

### Takeaway
A volume-crime (burglary) CSI kit is dominated by a latent-print module (2 oz powder jars, fibreglass/feather brushes, magnetic wand, 1.5–2 in × 360 in clear tape, hinge lifters 1.5×2 / 2×4 / 4×4 in, 3×5 in backing cards), a photography module (interchangeable-lens camera with macro, flash on 3 ft sync cord, tripod, ABFO No. 2 105 mm L-scale, 3–3.5 in yellow numbered tents), a DNA module (sterile cotton swabs, sterile water ampoule, double swab boxes, nitrile gloves, mask, Tyvek suit), casting (≈2–3 lb dental stone per shoe print, folding frame, 7 oz Mikrosil for tool marks), packaging (kraft bags 8×5×18 and 12×7×18 in, 3.2 mil tamper-evident poly bags 4×7.5 and 7.5×10.5 in, KAPAK 4.5 mil pouches), 3 in × 1000 ft barrier tape, 350 × 350 mm stepping plates, an ALS torch with orange goggles, and 500 ml / 125 ml BlueStar luminol kits. Hard dimensions exist for most consumables; several hand tools (brushes, wands, ALS torch bodies, scene tents, lamps) lack published dimensions in the sources reached.

**Photography module**
- NIST/OSAC's Standard Guide for Crime Scene Photography (OSAC Proposed, v1.0, June 2020) lists the recommended minimum kit: "Camera - Single Lens Reflex (SLR) or Mirrorless Interchangeable Lens Camera (MILC)", "Lenses covering normal to wide angle fields of view, and macro capabilities", a flash unit, "off-camera sync cord (3 feet) or a wireless trigger", a shutter release remote or self-timer, a tripod, and "scale/rulers"; documentation must record "the case or lab number, the photographer's name, the location, and the date", and "a photo of any document containing that information also satisfies the requirement" — [NIST OSAC Crime Scene Photography guide](https://nist.gov/system/files/documents/2020/06/26/Crime%20Scene%20Photography%20Doc_OSAC%20Proposed_June2020.pdf); [NIST document page](https://www.nist.gov/document/standard-guide-crime-scene-photography)
- A tripod "head or center shaft that can be mounted upside down" matters for shoe and tyre impressions; heavy DSLR bodies "call for a sturdy, heavy-duty head" — [Officer.com "Picture perfect"](https://www.officer.com/home/article/10249731/picture-perfect)
- Photo categories (secondary sources): overall photos "taken at normal height with lighting set as it was at the scene"; mid-range (evidence-establishing) shows "how items relate to each other and to the scene"; close-ups "with and without a scale" and "sensor parallel to the surface" — [Maine State Police Crime Scene Photography](https://www.maine.gov/dps/msp/sites/maine.gov.dps.msp/files/inline-files/Crime%20Scene%20Photography.pdf); [Ventura County Basic Evidence Photography bulletin](https://www.vcccd.edu/sites/default/files/media/pdf_document/2024/24-009%20Basic%20Evidence%20Photography_Redacted.pdf)

### Inferences
- For 3D modelling, the following can be treated as reliable "canonical" dimensions: powder jar = 2 oz (59 ml) screw-top jar; backing card = 3 × 5 in (76 × 127 mm); hinge lifter = 2 × 4 in dominant size; tape roll = 1.5 or 2 in wide (38/51 mm) × 30 ft; evidence tent = 3–3.5 in (76–89 mm) wide, 3 in tall, yellow with black numerals and 8 cm scale; ABFO scale = 105 × 105 mm L, 1 in legs; barrier tape = 3 in (76 mm) yellow with black text; stepping plate = 350 × 350 mm clear or aluminium; kraft bag = 8 × 5 × 18 in or 12 × 7 × 18 in; tamper-evident poly bag = 4 × 7.5 or 7.5 × 10.5 in; Mikrosil tube = 7 oz (toothpaste-tube form factor); BlueStar bottle = 500 ml with trigger sprayer or 125 ml Magnum. These are retailer/manufacturer figures, not independent measurements.
- Colour conventions evident across the catalogues: evidence tents yellow (white reverse), barrier tape yellow/black, Tyvek white, ALS goggles orange, viewing plates orange, dental stone buff (also blue/green dyed), Mikrosil brown/white/grey, kraft bags brown with black-printed chain-of-custody block, integrity bags clear with blue/red tamper strip (strip colour not confirmed by a source).
- A jewellery-store burglary scene will most heavily exercise the latent-print, footwear (gel lifter/electrostatic/casting), tool-mark (Mikrosil on pry marks), glass-handling (kraft bags/boxes) and DNA (swabs on touched surfaces, blood on broken glass) modules; the luminol and casting modules are secondary for an indoor shop.
- Brands to model from: Sirchie (black-bodied kits, "SEARCH" branding), Arrowhead Forensics, Tri-Tech Forensics, BVDA (Gellifters), Foster + Freeman (Crime-lite), BlueStar Forensic, Safariland/Lightning Powder (Mikrosil, stepping plates), Scenesafe (UK consumables).

## Key question 2 — How CSIs are trained and assessed: curricula, hours, competency frameworks, accreditation, mock-scene houses, common trainee errors

### Takeaway
CSI training is practical and competency-based: UK forces use College of Policing-approved initial courses of roughly 4–6 weeks (Stage 1/2), underpinned by Skills for Justice NOS units and the Forensic Science Regulator's Code (ISO/IEC 17020 for scene examination, with the April 2027 accreditation deadline suspended in 2026 in favour of milestones), while the US relies on the IAI CCSI (1 year experience + 48 h training + exam, 5-year cycle) and intensive academies such as the 10-week, ~400-hour National Forensic Academy. Documented trainee/first-responder error patterns centre on contamination (glove reuse, no PPE), under-photography, narrow scene boundaries, missing access logs and chain-of-custody breaks; mock-scene houses are the standard assessment venue.

- First responders "sometimes end up contaminating evidence while they wait for investigators"; scene boundaries "set too narrowly" — [Coroner Talk ep219](https://coronertalk.com/?p=3697); failure to log everyone who enters/leaves breaks chain of custody — [Coroner Talk](https://coronertalk.com/?p=4063)

### Inferences
- The assessable behaviours that recur across the rubrics and error literature — and which a VR scoring system can measure objectively — are: cordon size and entry/exit logging; PPE donned before entry and gloves changed between exhibits; photo sequence (overall → mid-range → close-up, with and without scale, camera perpendicular); correct exhibit numbering/tenting before recovery; packaging type matched to evidence (paper for wet/biological, poly tamper-evident for dry items, boxes for glass/sharps); sealing and labelling at the scene; a sketch with measured positions; and a final survey before release.
- UK initial CSI training (~4–6 weeks) is shorter and more volume-crime-focused (burglary, theft) than the US 10-week NFA model, which is weighted to serious crime; a jewellery-store burglary scenario maps directly onto the UK "Level 1/Stage 1 volume crime" curriculum and onto the Wetherby house's "burglaries, thefts" scenarios.
- FSR-GUI-0006's requirement that competency assessments "provide an equivalent scope and scale of challenge to the live operating environment" including lighting/weather is a direct design brief for a VR assessment scene (night lighting, broken glass, weather at the entry point).

### Gaps
- Official College of Policing CSI curriculum pages (course length, modules, assessment) were not retrievable; durations come from a Gibraltar news item and a private provider.
- The actual text of SFJCN403 performance criteria and the FSR competency framework for INC 100 were not read; only their existence and scope.
- IAI CCSI exam format (question count, pass mark) and recertification details were not confirmed from theiai.org (page blocked); only secondary sources.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that quantifies *trainee* error rates for over-powdering, wrong packaging or missed marks at mock scenes; the Studebaker-Reed and RTI studies are the closest, and their numeric results were not accessible.
- Sirchie's guidance on over-powdering/brush technique was not reached (search budget exhausted).

## Key question 3 — Existing virtual/VR/AR crime-scene trainers: what they simulate, evaluation findings, design lessons

### Takeaway
Published VR/AR/MR CSI trainers (UWE Bristol 2020, Hassenfeldt/Baggili 2020, Albeedan et al. 2023–24 HoloLens for Kuwait police, Abu Dhabi Police arson VR 2024, Keele virtual scenes, RTI/NIST national VR study 2025, College of Policing/Staffordshire PhD to Feb 2027) consistently report high satisfaction and learning equal to (not better than) physical training, faster completion in VR, minimal motion sickness in small samples but enough in a 163-person sample to exclude some users, and weakest scores on the "learning" construct; the most variable (hence most trainable/assessable) behaviours were DNA swabbing, glove use and photo count.

### Cited Findings
- **UWE Bristol (Mayne & Green 2020, Science & Justice 60(5):466–472, DOI 10.1016/j.scijus.2020.07.006)**: a bespoke VR crime scene app tested with undergraduate and staff/postgraduate cohorts; authors concluded "VR applications support learning of practical crime scene processing skills, and reports of adverse effects (motion sickness) were minimal"; motivated by "cost, accessibility, and size limitations of real-world crime scenes" — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340887673_Virtual_Reality_for_Teaching_and_Learning_in_Crime_Scene_Investigation); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1355030620300927). Sample size and numeric results were not visible in excerpts.
- **Hassenfeldt, Jacques & Baggili 2020 (DFRWS / FSI: Digital Investigation)**: 57 participants, lecture + lab on "bagging and tagging digital devices at a crime scene", randomly assigned to VR or physical groups with the same content; "Pre- and post-test scores showed no significant difference between the VR and physical groups"; "VR participants finished faster" — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10201308); [UNH Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.newhaven.edu/electricalcomputerengineering-facpubs/101)
- **Albeedan, Kolivand & Hammady 2024, *Virtual Reality* (Springer, DOI 10.1007/s10055-024-01018-8)**: mixed-reality CSI training on Microsoft HoloLens 2 "built over 3D-scanned crime scenes and aimed at trained Kuwaiti police officers"; "Forty-four young police officers evaluated the system" using the Questionnaire of User Interaction Satisfaction and User Experience Questionnaire; "positive levels of satisfaction… with minimal negative feedback"; "The overall impression construct receiving the highest score and the learning construct receiving the lowest score"; female students rated overall impression higher than males — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10055-024-01018-8); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381703381_Designing_and_evaluation_of_a_mixed_reality_system_for_crime_scene_investigation_training_a_hybrid_approach). - Earlier work: "Use of VR… a case study for teaching crime scene investigation" (Cardwell 2017, IATED) — [IATED](https://library.iated.org/view/CARDWELL2017USE); "Preparing and Guiding Forensic Crime Scene Inspections in Virtual Reality" (HS Flensburg) — [HS Flensburg publications](https://hs-flensburg.de/publikationen?author=155)

### Inferences
- Design lessons that follow from the evidence: (1) VR matches but does not beat physical training on knowledge tests, so its value case is throughput, repeatability, safety and cost (Hassenfeldt; Halford); (2) the "learning" construct scores lowest in MR satisfaction studies, so explicit feedback/debrief mechanics are needed, especially where no instructor is present (Albeedan; Halford's note that staff were absent and feedback relied on built-in mechanisms); (3) the behaviours that discriminate between agencies — glove changes, swabbing technique, number/sequence of photographs — are the ones a simulation should track and score (RTI/NIST); (4) prior VR experience is not a barrier, but a comfort/seated mode is needed for the motion-sick minority (RTI); (5) scoring by traces found improves with repetition (tagging game), so a repeatable scene with randomised evidence placement supports mastery learning; (6) students still prefer face-to-face when the virtual scene is a passive 360° tour (Keele), so interactivity (powdering, lifting, swabbing, bagging, photographing) is what distinguishes useful trainers from tours.
- A jewellery-store burglary scenario is well suited to VR because it exercises a dense, enumerable evidence set (glove/finger marks on display cases, footwear marks on glass/floor, tool marks on frames/safes, blood on glass, CCTV/EAS tags) that can be objectively scored for found/missed/contaminated.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Mayne & Green 2020, Wilkins et al. 2024, Albeedan et al. 2024 and Halford et al. 2024 were not accessible; sample sizes, instruments and numeric results beyond the quoted excerpts are unknown.
- No source gave a published VR assessment rubric (scoring weights for contamination, missed marks, photo quality) — only the behaviours measured.
- No evidence found on measured *skill transfer* from VR to real scenes (all studies measure tests, satisfaction or in-VR performance).
- NFI (Netherlands Forensic Institute) VR crime-scene work was referenced only in a truncated snippet; not confirmed.
- No details retrieved on commercial "virtual crime scene" apps or the ReliOn/other vendor products' CSI modules.

## Key question 4 — Gulf/Oman/GCC forensic training institutions and published curricula

### Takeaway
The UAE has the most visible GCC CSI training infrastructure: a Federal Crime Scene Village in Ajman running three-month "Crime Scene Forensic Investigation – Level 1" courses with a Scottish Police-linked diploma, Abu Dhabi Police's own CSI diploma with the Scottish Police College, and the Abu Dhabi Police-built VR arson trainer evaluated at the Saif Bin Zayed Academy; NAUSS (Riyadh) offers forensic higher diplomas/MSc/PhD and short courses for Arab security forces but no crime-scene-specific curriculum was found; nothing on Royal Oman Police CSI training was found beyond the existence of its Forensic Laboratory and the Sultan Qaboos Academy for Police Sciences.

### Inferences
- GCC CSI training is modelled on UK/Scottish Police College practice (Abu Dhabi and Ajman diplomas), so UK-style procedures (CSI Levels 1/2, exhibit labelling, FSR-type quality frameworks) are a defensible procedural baseline for an Oman-set simulation.
- The UAE and Kuwait already use VR/MR for police/CSI training, which suggests institutional appetite in the region for a VR jewellery-burglary trainer.

### Gaps
- No published Royal Oman Police CSI curriculum, course length or competency framework was found; ROP and Sultan Qaboos Academy pages were not reachable.
- No NAUSS crime-scene-specific diploma syllabus found; only general forensic programme descriptions.
- The Ajman diploma's current accreditation status and whether it still runs with Scottish Police involvement is from 2021 press items and a single source.
- Dubai Police Academy's own CSI curriculum was not found (only its visit to the Ajman village).
