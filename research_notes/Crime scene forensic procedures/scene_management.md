## Key Question 1 — First responder duties: preserving life, securing the scene, cordons (outer/inner/common approach path), scene log, who may enter, PPE and why (Locard)

### Takeaway
Every major standard gives the first officer the same ordered priorities: deal with threats and preserve life, treat the location as a crime scene until shown otherwise, fix a boundary that is generous and can shrink later, control and record every person who crosses it (a single scene log that itself becomes an exhibit), keep everyone to one controlled route (common approach path), and hand over to investigators with a written account of everything done. The justification is Locard's principle: every entry both removes and deposits trace material, so access and movement are the main variables the first responder can control.

**Why: Locard's exchange principle**
- [UK] College of Policing: anyone who enters a scene "both takes something of the scene away with them and leaves something of themselves behind" — [College of Policing APP, Forensics](https://www.college.police.uk/app/investigation/forensics)
- Short form "Every contact leaves a trace", generally understood as "with contact between two items, there will be an exchange"; credited to Edmond Locard — [Wikipedia, Locard's exchange principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locard's_exchange_principle)

### Inferences
- For a VR simulation the first-responder phase can be scored on a short, standards-backed checklist: (1) threat/safety sweep, (2) life-saving first, (3) note who is present/leaving and vehicles, (4) set an oversized boundary (APP's three-tier structure can be collapsed to outer + inner for a shop), (5) establish one access control point and one scene log, (6) define a CAP that avoids the offender's probable route (for a shop: the smashed shopfront/door and the route to the display cases), (7) record everything touched or moved, (8) hand over to CSI with the log. Each step is sourced above.
- UK and US sources agree the scene log is an evidential document (PSNI: "marked as an exhibit"); a sim should therefore treat a lost, duplicated or incomplete log as a failure state, not a cosmetic error.
- Oman's legal framework places scene protection on "judicial police" under Public Prosecution supervision, so an Oman-set scenario should include notifying the Public Prosecution and producing a signed report as explicit tasks, which differs from UK/US practice where the police investigator controls the scene.

### Gaps
- Exact APP wording on the common approach path and on scene-log contents was not retrievable (the APP page could not be fetched); the CAP definitions above come from training sources plus an FSR fragment.
- No source gave a numeric distance for cordon placement (e.g. "x metres from point of entry"); every source found treats boundary size as judgement ("generous, shrink later" is an inference from the NIJ/FSR language that boundaries may change).
- No source found that defines, in general terms, who the SIO/CSM may authorise to enter; this sits in force-level policy.
- Oman: no published Royal Oman Police scene procedure or first-responder checklist was found.

## Key Question 2 — Scene assessment and planning: walk-through, hypothesis, processing sequence (least intrusive first, photograph before touching, fragile/transient first), search patterns

### Takeaway
All standards insist on a documented assessment and strategy before anything is touched: an initial visual walk-through, a written (generic or bespoke) examination strategy that takes a "holistic view" and may challenge the brief, then processing in an order chosen so earlier techniques do not destroy later ones (transient evidence first; biological/trace recovery before powders or chemicals). Search patterns are a tool for systematic coverage, chosen by scene size, shape and staffing; for a shop interior the zone pattern is the natural fit, with the point of entry treated as the highest-yield area.

### Cited Findings

**Walk-through and strategy**
- [US] NIJ: the walk-through gives the lead investigator an overview of the whole scene and flags threats to its integrity; it "is the first opportunity to identify valuable and/or fragile evidence and determine initial investigative procedures"; where evidence is at risk, immediate documentation and collection may be needed before the walk-through so that "all evidence that may be compromised is documented, photographed and collected" — [NIJ 2013 guide](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/Crime-Scene-Investigation.pdf)
- [US] OSAC 2022-N-0025 requires the investigator to "conduct a scene assessment (also known as a 'walkthrough') using appropriate precautions, to obtain an overview of the entire scene" — [OSAC 2022-N-0025 v2.1](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2024/03/04/OSAC%202022-N-0025%20Standard%20for%20Scene%20Response%20Initial%20Response%20by%20Scene%20Investigators%20Version%202.1.pdf)
- [UK] FSR statutory Code v2 §90.5.1: "An initial assessment of the scene shall be carried out, and should: include identifying and preserving the scene as initially understood", noting that "the scene boundaries may change during examination"; documentation is to be proportionate, recorded promptly and in enough detail for later examiners to follow the rationale — [FSR Code of Practice version 2 (accessible)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/forensic-science-activities-statutory-code-of-practice-version-2/forensic-science-activities-statutory-code-of-practice-version-2-accessible)
- [UK] FSR Code v2 §90.6.1: "An examination strategy shall be documented" (standalone or within examination notes; may be generic for common incident types or bespoke for complex ones); §90.6.2: "The examination strategy shall take a holistic view of the incident scene" and "shall take into account and, where necessary, challenge the initial investigation requirements and information received" — [FSR Code of Practice version 2 (accessible)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/forensic-science-activities-statutory-code-of-practice-version-2/forensic-science-activities-statutory-code-of-practice-version-2-accessible)
- [UK] Draft 2025 Code (FSA INC 100, §17 "Developing an examination strategy") §17.1.1: "The purpose of an examination strategy is to ensure that the FSA, or suite of FSAs, being applied is appropriate to the investigative questions or evaluative opinion"; practitioners should capture the commissioning party's requirements and request task-relevant information — [FSR Code of Practice 2025 (laid before Parliament)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67daba1e594182179fe0883b/E03313596+-+CoP+Forensic+Science+Regulator+2025_A4_v02_Web+Accessible.pdf)

**Processing order**
- [US] NIJ principle: "prioritize the collection of evidence to prevent loss, destruction, or contamination"; the lead investigator and team determine collection order; "identify the most transient evidence and prioritize processing"; "collection of biological or trace evidence should be performed prior to use of powder or chemical enhancement techniques"; "monitor environmental and other factors that may affect evidence" and "reprioritize collection, if warranted, based on conditions" — [NIJ 2013 guide](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/Crime-Scene-Investigation.pdf)
- [US] Photography sequence: "overview photographs first, then mid-range photographs to show where evidence is, then close-up photographs to show evidence details" — [Maine State Police, Crime Scene Photography](https://www.maine.gov/dps/msp/sites/maine.gov.dps.msp/files/inline-files/Crime%20Scene%20Photography.pdf)

**Search patterns**
- Strip (lane): searchers walk parallel lanes boundary to boundary, offset one lane width and return; best for large outdoor areas. Grid: a strip search repeated at 90 degrees ("the area is searched twice in two perpendicular directions"). Spiral: one searcher; inward spiral for small confined areas, outward for scenes with no outer edge. Zone/quadrant: scene divided into sections, "often rooms or indoor areas", often combined with another pattern inside each zone. Wheel/ray: lines radiate from a centre; "may be used when time for searching is limited, such as when adverse weather conditions make it likely that the crime scene will soon be disturbed" — [EBSCO Research Starters, Crime scene search patterns](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/applied-sciences/crime-scene-search-patterns/); [Applied Police Resources](https://appliedpoliceresources.com/blogs/news/crime-scene-search-patterns); [forensicreader.com](https://forensicreader.com/crime-scene-search-methods/); [forensicspot.com](https://forensicspot.com/topics/crime-scene-management/crime-scene-search-techniques)
- Sources conflict on the wheel pattern's best use: one says large open spaces (fields, deserts), another says small circular scenes — [forensicfield.blog](https://forensicfield.blog/search-methods-used-in-crime-scene-investigation/) vs [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/applied-sciences/crime-scene-search-patterns/)
- Gardner lists circle/spiral, strip, grid, zone and point-to-point under assessment and search — [Taylor & Francis, Processing Methodology](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315170596-4/processing-methodology-ross-gardner-donna-krouskup)

**Burglary-specific examination (point of entry, glass, tool marks, footwear)**
- "the best evidence is usually found at the point of greatest activity, which at burglary scenes is often where entry was gained" — [Dutelle, Jones & Bartlett sample chapter 10 (Impression evidence)](http://samples.jbpub.com/9781284082852/9781284082852_CH10_Dutelle_SECURE.pdf)
- Locate the point of entry and examine it for tool marks, heel prints, fingerprints, blood, fibres and tissue; sample unusual dust (powdered putty, paint) for comparison — [Crime and Clues, Burglary Investigations](https://crimeandclues.com/2013/01/26/burglary-investigations/)
- "collecting samples from the crime scene is the first step in the glass examination and comparison process"; known reference samples are also needed; pieces of window or door glass lying on the ground can carry footwear impressions and should be preserved carefully — [Santa Barbara Sheriff SOP-CSI-004, Trace evidence collection](https://www.sbsheriff.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/SOP-CSI-004-12-Trace-Evidence-Collection.pdf)
- A toolmark is "any impression, cut, gouge or abrasion left by a tool contacting another object"; entry-point impressions usually show the class of tool and can sometimes be matched to a single tool; photograph tool/pry marks at close range with mid-range context shots — [Petraco, Impression Evidence: Footwear and Tool Marks (2016)](https://jjcweb.jjay.cuny.edu/npetraco/pubs/Impression_Evidence_Chapter_2016r.pdf); [Crime Scene and Evidence Photography ch. 5](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/CSEP/chapters/crime-scene-and-evidence-photography-2nd-edition-chapter5.pdf)
- Footwear impressions are often found below windows or on kicked doors and on the inside of broken glass; they can be lifted with powder and tape or photographed with a scale; compared via class/subclass characteristics, wear and accidental marks — [Dutelle ch. 10](http://samples.jbpub.com/9781284082852/9781284082852_CH10_Dutelle_SECURE.pdf); [Petraco 2016](https://jjcweb.jjay.cuny.edu/npetraco/pubs/Impression_Evidence_Chapter_2016r.pdf)
- Footwear marks must be protected because "walking over them can destroy them" (UK victim advice) — [Security Guides, What happens when the police attend a burglary](https://securityguides.co.uk/reporting-crime/after-burglary/police-attendance/) (commercial site)
- [US trade] Jewellery raiders "often cut themselves on small holes in laminated glass", leaving blood of DNA value — [Jewelers' Security Alliance via Southern Jewelry News](https://southernjewelrynews.com/press-release/jewelers-security-alliance-offers-smash-and-grab-robbery-recommendations/)
- [UK] Non-residential burglaries: fingermarks present at 45% of unscreened scenes (41% for dwelling burglary and vehicle crime); failures to yield evidence "were usually due to scenes being cleaned prior to examination" — [Home Office, The use of forensic science in volume crime investigations (HOOR 43/05)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7ad567e5274a34770e76f6/hoor4305.pdf)
- [UK] Surrey: "in 2017 the average time taken to examine the scene of a residential burglary was 1.5 hours. This has now risen to 3 hours", attributed to stricter regulation and additional recording requirements — [Surrey PCC response to HMICFRS "Finding time for crime"](https://www.surrey-pcc.gov.uk/statutory-response/commissioners-response-to-hmicfrs-report-the-police-response-to-burglary-robbery-and-other-acquisitive-crime-finding-time-for-crime/)
- [UK] Burglary-dwelling CSI attendance rates are "generally high" (around 85% in 2004/05) — [Home Office HOOR 43/05](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7ad567e5274a34770e76f6/hoor4305.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible processing order for a jewellery-store burglary, assembled from the NIJ sequencing rule and the burglary sources: (1) walk-through along the CAP, (2) overall photographs/video before anything is touched, (3) transient/fragile items first (blood on laminated glass, footwear marks on glass shards and floor, wet or windblown material), (4) biological/DNA swabs and trace (fibres, glass, paint) before any powdering, (5) fingerprint powder/chemical enhancement at the point of entry, display cases, tills and safes, (6) tool-mark casting/photography at the pry points, (7) collection of control/reference samples (glass, paint), (8) final survey. The sources support each step individually; the composite ordering is my synthesis.
- The zone pattern is the right teaching default for a shop interior (sources tie zone searches to rooms), with the point of entry and display cases as priority zones; a strip or grid pattern suits the exterior approach/escape route (pavement, car park) where dropped items and glass transfer are likely.
- UK scene times (1.5 to 3 hours for a dwelling burglary) give the designer a plausible real-time budget; a commercial scene with many display cases would run longer.

## Key Question 3 — Contamination control and anti-contamination: glove changes, stepping plates, masks, suits, elimination prints, DNA elimination database

### Takeaway
UK regulation is the most explicit: the FSR statutory Code requires PPE/barrier clothing on entering any scene or examination area and written rules on when it is changed, and the FSR's scene guidance (FSR-GUI-0016, Oct 2023) frames anti-contamination as prevention plus detection, with the three recognised transfer routes being person to exhibit, consumable to exhibit, and exhibit to exhibit. Detection relies on comparing recovered DNA against reference profiles of staff (police elimination databases); fingerprint elimination from legitimate occupants follows the same logic.

### Cited Findings
- [UK] FSR Code §103.5 (contamination avoidance): "requires PPE/barrier clothing when entering scenes and examination, recovery and processing areas, and procedures must minimise DNA transfer by defining when PPE is cleaned and/or changed" — [FSR Code of Practice (accessible, now marked withdrawn; superseded by v2)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/statutory-code-of-practice-for-forensic-science-activities/forensic-science-regulator-code-of-practice-accessible)
- [UK] FSR-GUI-0016 Issue 1 (Oct 2023, 38 pp.) "provides guidance on how to control and avoid the introduction of DNA, or biological material containing DNA, to an exhibit, particularly during scene examination"; covers "control avoidance and detection of DNA contamination at incident scenes where DNA evidence is being recovered"; "statutory requirements were removed from this document and can now be found in the statutory code of practice" (Code section 103) — [FSR-GUI-0016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/65362ef6e839fd000d867325/FSR-GUI-0016_DNA_contamination_controls_-_incident_scenes.pdf); landing page [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/crime-scene-dna-anti-contamination-guidance)
- [UK] FSR-GUI-0016 draws on ILAC G19, which calls for demonstrating that scene reagents and kits are fit for purpose, a cross-contamination risk assessment, and an assessment of each individual scene; it is to be read with PAS 377:2023 (forensic-grade consumables) and BS ISO 18385:2016 — [FSR-GUI-0016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/65362ef6e839fd000d867325/FSR-GUI-0016_DNA_contamination_controls_-_incident_scenes.pdf)
- [UK] Principal contamination sources: "personnel to exhibit/DNA sample, contaminated consumables to exhibit/DNA sample, and exhibit to exhibit or sample to sample"; anti-contamination measures fall into "two core areas of activity, the first being prevention and the second detection" — [FSR DNA contamination detection (accessible)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dna-contamination-detection/dna-contamination-detection-accessible)
- [UK, secondary] A review states that FSR-GUI-0016 sections 4.1.15 and 6.2 detail PPE usage requirements and section 4.2.3 covers cleaning of scene examination equipment (section numbers not verified against the PDF) — [Contamination Control and QA in Forensic DNA Analysis: UK and India (J Forensic Sci Med)](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jfsm/fulltext/10.4103/jfsm.jfsm_150_24~contamination-control-and-quality-assurance-in-forensic-dna)
- [UK, medical-examination analogue] FSR-GUI-0017 (forensic medical examinations): minimum of "two pairs of disposable non-latex powder-free gloves"; the outer pair "should be changed between sampling different areas; before handling equipment; or after touching frequently touched surfaces"; a face mask is "preferable", not mandatory — [FSR, DNA contamination controls: forensic medical examinations (accessible)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dna-contamination-controls-forensic-medical-examinations/dna-contamination-controls-forensic-medical-examinations-accessible); PDF [here](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/65a80392b2f3c6000de5d4c1/DNA_contamination_controls___Forensic_medical_examinations.pdf). (Healthcare setting; transfer to scenes is an inference.)
- [UK] Scene-level controls listed in the FSR scene guidance: record PPE worn by all persons with legitimate access (§4.1.5); control of entry to and exit from specific areas; examination of different rooms on different days or by different personnel; rendezvous point outside the cordon (§4.1.12) — [FSR-G-206 consultation draft](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/393866/206_FSR_SOC_contamination_consultation.pdf)
- [UK] Stepping plates: "in most cases stepping plates will be used along this path to save the evidence from being stepped on" (student notes); benefits listed in UK training flashcards: protect and cover evidence with minimum disruption, whereas walking on plain paper or plastic can shift or damage marks such as shoe impressions — [Studocu CSI 101 notes](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/loveless-academic-magnet-program-high-school/forensic-science/student-notes-to-print-crime-scene-processing/112951076); [Brainscape, Crime Scene Examination flashcards](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/crime-scene-examination-5696986/packs/8560733) (both weak sources; the FSR-GUI-0006 fragment above is the authoritative pointer)
- [UK] DNA elimination: "detection of inadvertent contamination primarily involves comparison of DNA profiles against personnel reference profiles" — [FSR-GUI-0019, Contamination detection: DNA elimination samples](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69959ce39f2f510ba1d0b87b/FSR-GUI-0019_Guidance_-_Contamination_Detection-_DNA_Elimination_Samples.pdf)
- [UK] DNA and fingerprints are used for "eliminating the innocent from investigations"; IDENT1 is the UK National Fingerprint Database holding arrestee prints and unidentified scene-of-crime marks — [FINDS-P-040 International DNA and Fingerprint Exchange Policy](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68a724192f18566482155758/FINDS-P-040Issue+5International+DNA+and+Fingerprint+Exchange+Policy+for+the+United+Kingdom__2.pdf)
- [UK] Victim-side anti-contamination advice: "Try not to touch anything that you think the offender may have touched, as this might interfere with potentially crucial evidence"; repairs or re-securing before the examination "may compromise any forensic examination" — [Essex Police burglary advice pack](https://www.essex.police.uk/police-forces/essex-police/areas/essex-police/au/about-us/burglary-advice-pack/); [Gloucestershire Constabulary burglary advice pack (2020)](https://www.gloucestershire.police.uk/SysSiteAssets/media/images/gloucestershire/html-email/2020-burglary-pack/2020-10-burglary-advice-pack.pdf)
- [US] NIJ Section C includes "contamination control" as a processing step (team composition, contamination control, documentation, prioritise collection, collect/preserve/package) — [NIJ 2013 guide](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/Crime-Scene-Investigation.pdf)
- Research context: a 2026 cross-sectional survey of practitioner perceptions of human factors in DNA contamination risk — [Bulletin of the National Research Centre](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s42269-026-01510-y); a mock-scene experiment on "reduced anticontamination measures" cites FSR-GUI-0016 and notes many scene contamination risks "have not been evaluated" — [Science & Justice (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1355030626000389)

### Inferences
- A sim's PPE model can follow the FSR logic: full barrier clothing on crossing the inner cordon; gloves changed at least between exhibits/areas and after touching equipment or common surfaces (the FME rule is the closest written UK rule; scene guidance is expected to be similar but was not readable); stepping plates laid along the CAP when floor marks are possible (a shop with a smashed door and glass on the floor is the textbook case).
- "Elimination prints/DNA" in a shop scenario means two populations: staff and owner (legitimate access, compared to exclude their marks) and police/CSI (held on personnel elimination databases). The FSR-GUI-0019 wording supports the second; the first is standard practice but no UK police source describing the victim/staff elimination process was found.

### Gaps
- The scene-specific PPE wording in FSR-GUI-0016 (suit, mask, overshoes, double gloving, when to change) could not be read; only pointers to its sections were found.
- No official source found describing how UK forces take elimination fingerprints/DNA from burglary victims or staff, or how such samples are destroyed afterwards.
- No source quantified a glove-change frequency at scenes (e.g. per exhibit) outside the FME guidance.

## Key Question 4 — Documentation: notes, sketch, photo log, exhibit log; chain of custody; exhibit labelling conventions (unique numbers, who/when/where, seals, tamper-evident bags, continuity)

### Takeaway
Documentation is layered (notes, photographs in three tiers, sketch with measurements, logs) and no layer substitutes for another; records must be contemporaneous or made at the first practical opportunity. Exhibit references in the UK are built from the seizing officer's initials plus a sequential number (the Home Office format adds the date), every tamper-evident bag's unique seal number is recorded, and each handover is logged so the item's continuity can be proved in court.

### Cited Findings
- [INT] Crime scene documentation "may include notes, photography, videos, sketches, measurements, or reports, and none of these substitutes for the others" (attributed to the UNODC manual by a secondary summary) — [UNODC manual](https://www.unodc.org/documents/scientific/Crime_scene_awareness__Ebook.pdf); summary in [Springer, Crime Scene Processing: Documentation and Evaluation](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-16-4091-9_2)
- [UK] FSR Code: technical records "should be produced contemporaneously or for incident scenes, at the first practical opportunity" — [FSR-GUI-0006 Issue 2](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a8709243d82f78d5c5145d3/FSR-GUI-0006_Incident_Scene_Examination_Issue_2.pdf)
- [US] Photography: overall shots first, then mid-range framed around a fixed landmark ("approximately ten to twenty feet from the subject matter"), then close-ups ("within about five feet"); for close-ups "two close-up images are standard: one captured with a labeled scale or ruler adjacent to the evidence, and a subsequent shot without the scale"; "always use a scale when taking photographs to show distance and size relationships" — [Maine State Police, Crime Scene Photography](https://www.maine.gov/dps/msp/sites/maine.gov.dps.msp/files/inline-files/Crime%20Scene%20Photography.pdf); [Police1, Crime scene imaging techniques](https://www.police1.com/police-products/investigation/forensic-supplies/articles/crime-scene-imaging-techniques-CCYCAqZYQi82Cxqe/); [L-Tron, types of photographs](https://www.l-tron.com/forensic-photography-what-types-of-photographs-are-taken-at-crime-scenes/)
- [US] Photo log fields: photographer, date and time, case number, camera and lens, lighting, exposure settings; "Because photography does have some inherent limitations, always create a crime scene sketch as an addendum to the photographic log"; "when using a digital camera, never delete a photograph from camera or digital media memory" — [crime-scene-investigator.net, Documentation of the crime scene outline](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/crime-scene-documentation-outline.pdf); [Crime Scene and Evidence Photography ch. 3](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/CSEP/chapters/crime-scene-and-evidence-photography-2nd-edition-chapter3.pdf)
- [US] Sketch measurement methods: triangulation uses "two fixed permanent objects" as reference points (bird's-eye view; useful outdoors); baseline method measures from a wall or measured line; a listed sketch scale is 1/4 inch = 1 foot for single rooms — [FBI (NCJRS 152997)](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/152997NCJRS.pdf); [crime-scene-investigator.net documentation outline](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/crime-scene-documentation-outline.pdf)
- [UK, Home Office] Reference format for seized items: three initials of the seizing officer ("with the last letter of the family name added if the officer has only two names"), the date as an 8-digit number, and a sequential number "that starts at 1 without leading zeros"; worked example "ABC25062023-1"; split items take the officer's initials plus a letter separated by a hyphen; "record the unique reference numbers of all TEBs to make sure you maintain a chain of evidence" — [Home Office, Tamper evident bags v7.0 (18 Sept 2025)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/951472/tamper-evident-bags-v7.0-gov-uk.pdf); accessible version [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/tamper-evident-bags/tamper-evident-bags-accessible-version). (Written for Immigration Enforcement/Home Office investigators; police forces use their own variants.)
- [UK, anecdotal] A forensic practitioners' forum describes the common police convention of initials/slash/number (e.g. "ABC/1") and asks whether variants cause continuity problems — [Forensic Focus, UK exhibit reference convention](https://www.forensicfocus.com/forums/general/uk-exhibit-reference-convention/)
- [UK, secondary] Exhibit label practice: the seizing officer signs the label with name and signature, location, date and time, and assigns the exhibit number; the exhibits officer then logs the item and signs the label — [Writing Crime, Packaging exhibits](https://rebeccabradleycrime.com/2018/05/21/writing-crime-packaging-exhibits/)
- [UK] "An exhibit register is an electronic master record of all exhibits in an investigation"; chain-of-custody forms are also kept (training flashcards) — [Brainscape, Exhibit management](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/exhibit-management-6579814/packs/10418865)
- [UK] Example of a published exhibit-management policy (Crown Prosecution Service-side) — [CW POL 16 Exhibit Management v2.0 (justice.gov.uk)](https://cdn.websitebuilder.service.justice.gov.uk/uploads/sites/5/2025/04/CW-POL-16-Exhibit-Management-v2.0.pdf)
- Tamper-evident seals: unauthorized attempts to open the bag make the word "STOP" appear in the seal strip — [Sirchie, Evidence packaging, labeling and sealing](https://www.sirchie.com/media/resourcecenter/item/0/5/05_evidence_packaging_labeling_sealing.pdf); UK CJA labels carry "Exhibit Continuity panels" for successive signatures, and card tags on string "can be lost, torn, or replaced", which affects continuity — [SceneSafe, Tamper-evident CJA exhibit label](https://scenesafe.co.uk/pages/new-tamper-evident-cja-exhibit-label-from-scenesafe) (vendor)
- Seal practice: after sealing, the officer writes initials and date across the seal so any later break damages the writing — [iSell Packaging, How to label and seal evidence bags](https://isellpackaging.com/how-to-label-and-seal-evidence-bags-complete-step-by-step-guide/) (commercial guide)
- [UK, NI] The scene log is itself an exhibit with continuity maintained — [PSNI SI0616](https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2023-11/Serious%20Crime%20Scenes%208%20September%202021.pdf)
- [US] NIJ: a secure area for temporary evidence storage "in accordance with rules of evidence and chain of custody" — [NIJ 2013 guide](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/Crime-Scene-Investigation.pdf)
- [INT] A first responder "must be able to testify later which steps were taken and explain any unavoidable alteration to the evidence" (electronic evidence guide, same continuity principle) — [ENISA, Electronic evidence: a basic guide for first responders](https://syntheticdrugs.unodc.org/uploads/syntheticdrugs/res/library/cybercrime_html/Electronic_Evidence_A_Basic_Guide_for_First_Responders_ENISA.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the sim, an exhibit record can be modelled as: unique reference (CSI initials + running number, e.g. "JS/1"), description, exact location found (sketch coordinate / photo number), date-time, name of person recovering, seal number of the tamper-evident bag, and a continuity chain of every handover with signature and time. Each element is supported above; the exact field set varies by force.
- "Photograph before touching", "scale and no-scale pairs" and "never delete a frame" are the three photo rules most likely to trip trainees and are easy to assess automatically in VR.

### Gaps
- No national UK police document setting the exhibit-reference convention or the wording of a continuity (MG11-style) statement was found; the Home Office TEB guide is the only official format located.
- Did not find a source for the UNODC manual's own documentation checklist text; the "none substitutes" wording comes via a secondary citation.
- Could not confirm SWGIT/IAI/ENFSI photography standards' specific requirements; distances (10 to 20 ft, 5 ft) are from a state police guide and a trade article, not a standard.

## Key Question 5 — Scene release: what must be done before handing back, dealing with the business owner, what is left behind

### Takeaway
Release is a formal, documented decision taken only after a final survey (all evidence and equipment accounted for, hidden areas rechecked, documentation checked for errors) and a team debrief; the record shows time/date, who released and to whom. For a shop owner the practical consequences are powder/chemical residue to clean, a damaged entry point they were told not to repair until examination, and statutory victim information (UK: Victims' Code leaflet, updates every 28 days per the Met).

### Cited Findings
- [US] "Final survey of the crime scene ensures that evidence has been collected and the scene has been processed prior to release" — [NIJ, Crime Scene Investigation: A Reference for Law Enforcement Training (NCJ 200160)](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/200160.pdf)
- [US] Final survey checklist: "Check to ensure all evidence is accounted for before departing scene. Ensure all equipment used in the search is gathered"; recheck hidden or hard-to-reach areas; review documentation for errors — [NIJ NCJ 200160](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/200160.pdf); [crime-scene-investigator.net, Crime scene response guidelines](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/respon3.html)
- [US] The debriefing "lets law enforcement personnel and other responders share information about particular scene findings before the scene is released", covering observations made, evidence collected, forensic testing and other actions needed to complete the investigation — [NIJ NCJ 200160](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/200160.pdf)
- [US] Two debriefs: "One occurs on-scene before releasing, relinquishing the scene and is with the entire crime scene crew. A formal debriefing occurs within 24 hours of completion of the scene processing" — [crime-scene-investigator.net, Response guidelines](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/respon3.html)
- [US] "At minimum, documentation should be made of time and date of release, to whom released, and by whom released"; NIJ: the scene is released "in accordance with jurisdictional requirements" — [crime-scene-investigator.net, Response guidelines](https://www.crime-scene-investigator.net/respon3.html); [NIJ 2013 guide](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/Crime-Scene-Investigation.pdf)
- [US] NIJ Section D ("completing and recording the crime scene investigation") comprises debriefing, final survey and documentation — [NIJ 2013 guide](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/forensics/Crime-Scene-Investigation.pdf)
- [EU] ENFSI generalist stages end with "completing the scene" and "review and closure" — [ENFSI AFORE WP2 accreditation BPM](https://enfsi.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/AFORE-WP2-BPM_Accreditation-SoC-1.pdf)
- [UK] What the victim is told to expect: a forensic examiner "may attend separately from the officer taking the report, sometimes the same day and sometimes later"; examination includes "Examining points of entry for fingerprints, tool marks and traces", "Powder or chemical treatment of surfaces, which leaves residue you will need to clean afterwards", "Swabbing for DNA where somebody may have made contact", footwear marks and photography; "ask the examiner what is safe to clean and when" — [Security Guides, Police attendance after a burglary](https://securityguides.co.uk/reporting-crime/after-burglary/police-attendance/) (commercial site, consistent with police packs)
- [UK] Do not repair or re-secure before the examination; "repairs or re-securing the home may compromise any forensic examination" — [Gloucestershire Constabulary burglary advice pack](https://www.gloucestershire.police.uk/SysSiteAssets/media/images/gloucestershire/html-email/2020-burglary-pack/2020-10-burglary-advice-pack.pdf)
- [UK] The investigating officer should give the victim their contact details and the leaflet "Information for victims of crime" (Victims' Code entitlements); the Met says victims "will be kept informed on how your case is progressing every 28 days" — [Met Police victim information leaflet](https://www.met.police.uk/SysSiteAssets/media/downloads/met/victim-information.pdf); [College of Policing, Residential burglary: further investigation](https://www.college.police.uk/guidance/residential-burglary/further-investigation)
- [US trade] Jewellers' Security Alliance advice: staff should not resist (suspects may carry guns, knives or sledgehammers); keep a log of suspicious incidents and save related CCTV; eye-level cameras including car-park coverage because ceiling cameras "often capture only the tops of heads" — [AGTA, JSA tips](https://agta.org/security-series-jewelers-security-alliance-tips-for-retailers-to-thwart-in-store-crime/); [Southern Jewelry News, JSA smash-and-grab recommendations](https://southernjewelrynews.com/press-release/jewelers-security-alliance-offers-smash-and-grab-robbery-recommendations/)
- [UK trade] SaferGems (jewellery trade crime scheme) advises staff training for three phases: spotting reconnaissance beforehand, complying during a robbery, and preserving evidence afterwards — [Professional Jeweller, SaferGems advice after Argyll Arcade theft](https://www.professionaljeweller.com/safergems-offers-advice-after-argyll-arcade-theft/) (c. 2014)

### Inferences
- A release gate for the sim: final survey checklist passed; debrief recorded; exhibits reconciled against the exhibit log and photo log; all plates, markers, kit removed; residue and damage explained to the owner; release time/date, releasing officer and recipient written in the scene log. All elements are sourced above; the gate itself is a design synthesis.
- Fingerprint powder on display cases, chemical treatment on the door/glass edges, and swab wrappers/markers left behind are the plausible "what remains" list; only the powder/chemical residue point is explicitly sourced.

### Gaps
- No UK source found stating who authorises release at a volume-crime (burglary) scene (CSI vs. officer in case) or at a major scene (SIO/CSM); the US sources defer to "jurisdictional requirements".
- No source found on handing a commercial premises back (insurer, inventory of stolen stock, boarding-up); only residential victim material was located.

## Key Question 7 — Common trainee errors and how training programmes assess scene-management competence

### Takeaway
Error literature attributes most scene mistakes to haste and lack of training/experience, with scene security, PPE use, unauthorised access, contamination, poor packaging and broken continuity as the recurring failure modes. Competence is assessed against documented frameworks (OSAC/ASTM training standard; UK FSR competence requirements) using staged checks, written/oral tests, and above all mock scenes with known outcomes, because the FSR says table-top exercises cannot demonstrate initial competence in scene approach.

### Cited Findings
- [US] OSAC/ASTM proposed "Standard Practice for Crime Scene Investigator Training, Continuing Education, Professional Development, Certification and Accreditation" (2018): trainee progress "should be checked at set intervals, using methods such as oral and written exams, exercises that mirror real casework, and reviews of mock casework", with passing criteria on competency tests; "Current practitioners may not need a newly designed program, but it is recommended that they undergo competency testing when new competencies are added"; model criteria set "a minimum of an associate's degree (with at least 8 hours of physical science)" and "strongly recommend a bachelor's degree"; it "provides foundational requirements for the training, continuing education, professional development, certification, and accreditation of crime scene investigators/unit(s)" — [NIST/OSAC Standard Practice for CSI training (2018 draft)](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2018/11/09/standard_practice_for_crime_scene_investigator_training_continuing_education_professional_development_certification_and_accreditation.pdf); status: proposed standard sent to ASTM for public comment — [NIST OSAC CSI subcommittee update 2019](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2019/04/03/nist_osac_csi_overview_update1.pdf)
- [UK] FSR-GUI-0006 Issue 2: "a table-top/isolated method of demonstrating competency would not be appropriate for some aspects of initial competence, such as scene approach, which would require examination of a scene with known outcomes"; the initial assessment and examination strategy "should be tested in practice" — [FSR-GUI-0006 Issue 2](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a8709243d82f78d5c5145d3/FSR-GUI-0006_Incident_Scene_Examination_Issue_2.pdf); competence is addressed at Code section 27 and 90.2 — [FSR-GUI-0006 Issue 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/683d93c5d23a62e5d32680ac/FSR-GUI-0006+Incident+Scene+Examination+Issue+1+.pdf)
- [UK] Version 2 of the FSR Code requires scene-examination organisations to have "a strong competency framework" — [FSR IESG minutes, 1 Apr 2025](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68654dd3e6557c544c74db5a/IESG+2025+04+01+-+Minutes.pdf)
- A review of US National Academy of Sciences findings attributes scene mistakes, "especially in specimen identification and collection", to "a combination of too much haste at scenes and the lack of experience/knowledge and/or professional training"; errors in processing scenes "can lead to poor samples and, in some cases, wrongful convictions" ("miscarriages of justice and wrongful imprisonments") — [San Jose State University, Themis: Minimum education requirements for crime scene investigators](https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1055&context=themis)
- Blog-level error lists (useful as checklists, not peer-reviewed): failing to secure the scene early; "non-use of gloves and other personal protective equipment (PPE) and unauthorized access"; contamination; poor packaging ("wet biological samples sealed in plastic"); broken chain of custody; overlooked trace evidence — [LIFS, Common mistakes made during crime scene investigations](https://lifs.co.in/blog/common-crime-scene-investigation-mistakes.html); [Forensic Bite (July 2026)](https://www.forensicbite.in/2026/07/common-mistakes-during-crime-scene.html?m=1)
- Kelty et al. critical-skills framework for top crime scene examiners (seven clusters, 2023 revision): work-life approach and stress resilience; cognitive abilities; communication and interpersonal skills; a holistic knowledge base; life and work experience; professional demeanour; approach to work; the 2024 paper maps each cluster to recruitment assessment methods (psychological testing, interview questions) — [Kelty, Ribaux & Robertson 2023, WIREs Forensic Science](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wfs2.1494); [Kelty, Joshua & Robertson 2024](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wfs2.1519); original 2011 study (knowledge, life experience, professionalism, approach to life, communication, cognitive abilities, stress management) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241727890_Professionalism_in_Crime_Scene_Examination_The_Seven_Key_Attributes_of_Top_Crime_Scene_Examiners)

- Human factors in contamination: a 2026 practitioner survey on human factors contributing to DNA contamination risk — [Bulletin of the National Research Centre](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s42269-026-01510-y); an experimental mock-scene study of reduced anti-contamination measures — [Science & Justice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1355030626000389)

### Inferences
- The FSR's "scene with known outcomes" requirement is a direct argument for a VR simulator: a VR scene has fully known ground truth (every planted mark, every contamination event), which lets an assessor score approach, CAP choice, order of recovery, PPE changes and record-keeping objectively.
- A scoring rubric for the sim can map each recurring error to an observable event: boundary set too small or late; person admitted without log entry; glove not changed between exhibits; item touched before photographed; powder applied before swabbing; wet item in plastic; exhibit without unique reference or seal number; release without final survey. Each maps to a sourced rule above.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study quantifying error rates by error type at real or mock scenes was found; the "haste and inexperience" attribution is qualitative.
- No published College of Policing CSI assessment rubric or national occupational standard text was retrieved.
- The final published ASTM version (if any) of the OSAC training standard was not confirmed; the 2018 document is a proposed standard.
